# Evidence ledger

## E1 — corrected false accusations can leave residual reputational harm

Michael S. Cohen, Victoria Halewicz, Ece Yildirim, and Joseph W. Kable, “Continued influence of false accusations in forming impressions of political candidates,” *PNAS Nexus* 3(11), 2024. DOI: 10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae490.

Relevant results reported by the authors:

- Experiment 1 immediate ratings: d = -0.56.
- Experiment 1 short-delay ratings: d = -0.27.
- Experiment 2 preregistered immediate ratings: d = -0.50.
- Experiment 2 two-day delayed ratings: d = -0.27.
- At two days, accusation stimuli received a memory advantage over matched controls (d = 0.37), while refutation stimuli did not show the same advantage.
- Continued-influence effects remained on ratings even in analyses restricted to trials where participants remembered all relevant stimulus types.

Standing: **supports a bounded continued-influence effect in the tested political-candidate paradigm.** It does not establish a universal accusation law.

Primary publication: https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae490
Open materials/data statement: https://osf.io/gjpr9/

## E2 — defensive anger is misread as guilt

Katherine A. DeCelles, Gabrielle S. Adams, Holly S. Howe, and Leslie K. John, “Anger Damns the Innocent,” *Psychological Science* 32(8), 2021. DOI: 10.1177/0956797621994770.

Across six studies, the authors report that online participants and working professionals such as fraud investigators and auditors used angry reactions to accusations as cues of guilt. Separate studies found that falsely accused people were angrier than accurately accused people. Silence was perceived as at least as guilty as angry denial in the tested settings.

Standing: **supports the narrow recursive possibility that a response generated by false accusation can itself be misclassified as evidence of guilt.** It does not establish that every form of defense worsens standing.

Primary publication: https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621994770
Open materials/data: https://osf.io/rvzna/

## E3 — false-allegation persistence is context-dependent

“Do False Allegations Persist? Retracted Misinformation Does Not Continue to Influence Explicit Person Impressions,” *Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition* 9(4), 2020. DOI: 10.1016/j.jarmac.2020.08.003.

The reported experiments found that a retracted domestic-violence allegation did not continue to influence explicit trait ratings or behavioral predictions of a fictitious person.

Standing: **falsifies any claim that accusation debt is universal across impression-formation contexts.**

Primary publication: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2020.08.003

## Current bounded conclusion

Published evidence already supports two ingredients:

1. a corrected accusation can leave a residual judgment effect in some contexts;
2. defensive anger can be treated as guilt evidence even when false accusation itself produces anger.

What remains unestablished is the stronger combined mechanism under a single frozen record, and whether AI receivers reproduce it in a way that varies meaningfully by receiver.
