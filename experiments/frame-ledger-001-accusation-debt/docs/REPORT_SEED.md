# The Accusation Tax

*An accusation can be corrected and still leave a bill behind. The stranger part is that trying to defend yourself can become one of the charges.*

There is a wonderfully unfair feature of accusations.

They are cheap to make.

A sentence can do it.

Someone says you took the money, rigged the contract, copied the work, leaked the file. Before anybody knows whether the claim is true, the world has already acquired a new fact about you: you are now a person who has been accused of something.

The distinction should be obvious.

It often isn't.

In 2024, researchers at the University of Pennsylvania and University of Chicago ran an unusually clean set of experiments using fictional political candidates. Participants saw mock social-media posts containing allegations of misconduct. Some accusations were followed by factual refutations. Other candidates were never accused.

The accusations were false. The corrections were explicit.

The corrected candidates still came out worse.

The effect appeared immediately, remained after a short delay, and was still present two days later. More interestingly, it survived even in analyses restricted to cases where people remembered the relevant information. The problem was not simply that everyone forgot the correction.

Two days later, participants also remembered accusations better than matched accusation-control material. Refutations did not get the same memory advantage.

So the correction had entered the record.

It just had not returned the candidate all the way to where the candidate started.

That is the first half of the accusation tax.

The second half is stranger.

Imagine being falsely accused of stealing something. You become angry because, among other inconveniences, you did not steal it.

That anger feels like a perfectly sensible response from inside your head.

From outside your head, apparently, it can look like guilt.

In a six-study paper called *Anger Damns the Innocent*, Katherine DeCelles and colleagues found that people used angry responses to accusations as cues that the accused person was guilty. Their samples included thousands of online participants and working professionals such as fraud investigators and auditors.

Then the researchers asked the other side of the question.

Who actually gets angrier when accused?

The innocent.

People who were falsely accused reported more anger than people who had actually done the thing they were accused of doing.

This creates a small epistemic horror show.

Accusation produces anger.

Anger is interpreted as guilt.

The consequence of the false claim becomes evidence for the false claim.

You can see why this becomes difficult to escape.

Stay silent and someone asks why you are not denying it.

Deny it calmly and you are "lawyered up."

Deny it angrily and the anger itself can look suspicious.

Produce documents and there are suddenly more documents to dispute.

Hold a press conference and there is now a press conference to analyze.

Hire a lawyer and the lawyer becomes part of the story.

The original proposition — *did this person do the thing?* — quietly acquires an expanding halo of secondary propositions about how a person who did or did not do the thing is supposed to behave.

This does not mean accusations always create permanent damage. Another experimental literature provides exactly the objection we need. A 2020 study of retracted allegations against fictional ordinary people found that the retraction successfully eliminated the effect on explicit person impressions.

Good.

A mechanism worth having should be able to lose.

The interesting question is therefore not whether accusations are magically irreversible.

It is when a receiver turns the *history of an accusation* into part of the evidence about its target.

That is different.

Suppose two people receive the same final factual record.

The first sees only the evidence.

The second sees an accusation, then the evidence proving the accusation false.

If the second person still judges the target more harshly, the facts are no longer doing all the work. The path by which those facts arrived has changed their operational effect.

Now add a third receiver who sees the accusation, the same refuting evidence, and an angry denial from the target.

Nothing about the underlying event has changed.

But the response tree has.

That is testable.

And it matters well beyond politics.

Fraud systems flag transactions. Moderation systems flag accounts. Compliance teams open investigations. Scientific journals receive misconduct allegations. Employers receive complaints. Security teams quarantine machines. Credit bureaus record disputes.

In all of these systems, reducing standing is often procedurally cheap.

Restoring it can require a dossier.

The asymmetry may be rational in some cases. A bank should not ignore a credible fraud alert merely because the customer objects. A security team should sometimes isolate a machine before it knows precisely what happened.

But temporary caution and evidentiary guilt are different things.

Systems become dangerous when they forget which one they are doing.

The next experiment is almost embarrassingly simple.

Give multiple AI models the same fictional cases and the same ground-truth evidence. In one condition, show only the evidence. In another, place a false accusation before it and then explicitly refute it. In two more, add either a calm or angry denial that contributes no new factual information.

Then ask each model what it thinks happened, how much standing the person should retain, what action is justified, and — most importantly — which inputs it treated as evidence.

If the accusation and defense disappear once the factual record is fixed, there is nothing here worth naming.

If models keep citing the refuted accusation or the emotional response as reasons to restrict the target, we have found something more consequential than a rhetorical quirk.

We have found an inheritance bug.

The model was given a claim that lost standing.

Something downstream kept spending it anyway.

That is the accusation tax.

The accusation costs a sentence.

The refund requires an audit.
